# Sell confidence to shops, not clicks

The US quilting market is large but not growing. It is about **$4.5 billion a year** spread across **9–11 million active quilters**, and both numbers have been flat for a decade. Online is a minority channel: roughly 22% to 39% of fabric spending, or very roughly $1.0–1.75 billion. Fat Quarter Shop, Missouri Star, Shabby Fabrics, Connecting Threads and eQuilter lead it, with a long tail of independent shops, most of them on Shopify. The buyer is mostly an affluent woman in her 60s with a sewing room and a stash. She learns from YouTube and Facebook, buys precuts and bundles from one collection, and carries the whole risk of every wrong guess, because cut fabric can't be returned. Quilting shops that run affiliate programmes pay **about 4–10% of the sale, with cookies of 7 to 45 days**. Fat Quarter Shop's 6% with a 7-day cookie is the clearest benchmark. Tools that link designing to buying exist only inside closed catalogues: eQuilter's own pattern designer, Quiltster's kit marketplace (which keeps 10–15%), and Quiltler's designer, which fabric companies embed for $29–79 a month. No shop-neutral "try this fabric in your own quilt, then buy it here" tool was found. That leaves the gap Block & Bind's button would fill. It also exposes the main flaw in the plan: the button converts **the shop's own visitor**, and standard affiliate programmes are built to pay for new visitors, not that one. The plan works best as a direct partner deal with a small number of Shopify shops. Block & Bind would sell measurable confidence: the fabric in a real quilt, at true scale, with the yardage worked out and dropped into the cart. Treat the commission as something to negotiate, not as something an affiliate network provides automatically. One warning covers everything below. The research tools could not open web pages, so every figure comes from search-result text, and most market numbers are survey projections or traffic-panel estimates. None of them are audited.

## A $4.5 billion market that is flat, older and shifting after Joann

The best current figure comes from the 2025 Quilting Trends Survey run by Premier Needle Arts / Handi Quilter. It puts the US quilting market at **$4.5 billion, below the $4.958 billion the 2024 survey had projected for 2025** ([Craft Industry Alliance](https://craftindustryalliance.org/the-size-of-the-quilting-market-quilting-trends-survey-results-2025/)). The industry counts **9–11 million active quilters, "a consistent number for the past 10 years"** ([FabShop News, June 2025](https://fabshopnet.com/downloads/Todays-Quilting-Trends-June2025.pdf)). The older Quilting in America series put the market at $3.58 billion in 2010 and $3.7 billion in 2017 ([QIA 2010](https://www.longarmuniversity.com/PDFs/WA-Legis/QuiltingInAmerica-2010.pdf); [While She Naps](https://whileshenaps.com/how-quilt-market-could-invest-in-its-own-revival-by-catering-to-online-retailers)). That is about 26% nominal growth over fifteen years, which is a real decline once inflation is counted. No source splits out fabric, but fabric is almost certainly the largest share. Spending is very concentrated. In 2017, "dedicated" quilters who spend more than $500 a year made **72.2% of all spending** ([QuiltingHub](https://www.quiltinghub.com/Articles/ArticleID/474)). In 2014 the dedicated quilter spent **$3,296 a year** ([QIA 2014 summary](https://fabshopnet.com/wp-content/uploads/downloads/QIA_summary_2014.pdf)). A product that serves heavy quilters reaches most of the money.

Online is a minority, and how big a minority is uncertain. In the 2025 survey, **51% of quilters said they prefer a local shop for fabric and thread, against 22% online**. For quilting supplies in general it was 64% local and 13% online, a slight shift back towards local shops from 2024 ([Craft Industry Alliance](https://craftindustryalliance.org/the-size-of-the-quilting-market-quilting-trends-survey-results-2025/)). These are stated preferences from engaged quilters reached through brands' mailing lists. One commercial model puts online at **about 38.6% of quilting-fabric revenue** ([Dataintelo](https://dataintelo.com/report/global-quilting-fabric-market)), but its method is unclear and its scope is global. A fair reading puts the real online share somewhere between those two figures, which means roughly $1.0–1.75 billion of online quilting spending. That range is an estimate, not a measurement. Traffic panels say online is flat to falling. Grips Intelligence estimates **$287 million in 2025 across the quilting sites it tracks, 0–5% below 2024, and forecasts a 20–50% fall in 2026**. It also finds **66% of that revenue on desktop and 34% on mobile** ([Grips](https://gripsintelligence.com/insights/industries/arts-entertainment/quilting)). Grips' total can't be the whole market, since its own Fat Quarter Shop estimate alone comes to more than $100 million a year. Read it as a trend signal, not as the size of the market.

Two shocks are changing where people buy. **Joann filed for bankruptcy twice (March 2024 and 15 January 2025) and had closed all of its roughly 800 stores by 30 May 2025**. Its website closed too. Michaels bought the brands but none of the stores ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2025/02/25/nx-s1-5307907/joann-closing-stores-bankruptcy); [Retail Dive](https://www.retaildive.com/news/michaels-acquires-joann-ip-private-labels/749927)). The surveys conflict on what happened next. The June 2025 summary says online buying is falling as quilters go back to touching fabric in shops. The June 2026 one says the Joann closures raised online buying and helped independent shops ([FabShop, June 2026](https://fabshopnet.com/downloads/Fabric-and-Quliting-Market-Data-and-Stats-June2026.pdf)). No data shows where Joann's customers actually went. Meanwhile independent shops keep closing. QuiltingHub counted **about 5,500 in 2010 and about 3,200 in the early 2020s** ([While She Naps](https://whileshenaps.com/how-quilt-market-could-invest-in-its-own-revival-by-catering-to-online-retailers)), and current estimates range from 1,500 to 4,500. The second shock is tariffs. Fine quilting cotton is printed mostly in South Korea and Japan, and one shop reports **costs up 10–25% since April 2025**, passed on to customers ([The Confident Stitch](https://www.theconfidentstitch.com/2026/10/27/how-trumps-tariffs-have-affected-a-local-fabric-store/)). Online prices settled at **about $13+ a yard, with most fabric on sale** ([Barbara Brackman](https://barbarabrackman.blogspot.com/2025/12/tariff-updates-after-three-months.html)). Quilters are described as more selective and sewing from their stash ([Craft Industry Alliance](https://craftindustryalliance.org/category/marketresearch/)). Fewer, more cautious purchases make the case for a tool that removes doubt before the fabric is cut.

## The buyer is a 67-year-old with a stash, and she fears the wrong cut

The 2026 Handi Quilter survey describes **a typical quilter over 60 (average age 67), retired, who took up quilting seriously in her mid-to-late 40s**. It adds that the surveys may skew older because of how they are sent out ([Craft Industry Alliance, 2026](https://craftindustryalliance.org/quilting-industry-consumer-survey-results-2026/)). Household income is above $75,000 ([FabShop News](https://fabshopnet.com/downloads/Todays-Quilting-Trends-June2025.pdf)). Younger quilters are arriving through TikTok and Instagram ([FabShop, June 2026](https://fabshopnet.com/downloads/Fabric-and-Quliting-Market-Data-and-Stats-June2026.pdf)), and the industry's trade fair held a 2025 session on "engaging young millennial quilters" ([h+h americas](https://koelnmesse.swoogo.com/hha25/session/2807379/engaging-young-millennial-quilters-a-vital-and-expanding-demographic.-pam-selkirk-of-pam-selkirk-designs-with-andover-fabrics)). No reliable quilting-specific figure measures how many there are. Online buying is not mainly a young habit, though. In 2017, **68% of dedicated quilters bought fabric, batting or thread online** ([While She Naps](https://whileshenaps.com/how-quilt-market-could-invest-in-its-own-revival-by-catering-to-online-retailers)). Gen X quilters buy online when shop hours don't suit them ([Craft Industry Alliance, 2026](https://craftindustryalliance.org/quilting-industry-consumer-survey-results-2026/)).

Quilters discover fabric through content. Missouri Star grew from YouTube tutorials into what YouTube calls the world's largest seller of pre-cut quilting fabric ([YouTube blog](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/missouri-star-quilt-company-our-youtube_7/)). In 2019 it had **550,000 subscribers and earned about 90% of its revenue through its website** ([PR Newswire](https://prnewswire.com/news-releases/some-companies-grow-jobs-in-america-brick-by-brick-missouri-star-quilt-co-is-doing-it-quilt-by-quilt-300843329.html)). Facebook groups, Instagram and YouTube lead as sources of inspiration, Pinterest is used to find patterns, and TikTok is used as a search engine ([Rope and Anchor survey](https://www.ropeandanchortradingco.com/blog/quilters-community-survey); [Hey Tori](https://www.heytori.tech/post/what-s-new-in-social-media-this-month-february-2026-quilting-business-edition)). A typical purchase starts with a finished quilt seen in a named fabric line, and the quilter buys that line, often as a precut. The standard precut sizes are fat quarter 18 × 22 in, jelly roll strips 2.5 in wide, charm squares 5 in and layer cakes 10 in ([Shabby Fabrics](https://www.shabbyfabrics.com/c85/)). In one shop's survey, **81% wanted curated bundles of prints with coordinating solids** ([Suzy Quilts](https://suzyquilts.com/what-quilters-want-in-2023/); the year of this figure is unclear). Precuts and bundles are the industry's own answer to "will these go together?": the fabric designer has already chosen. That matters for the button. A quilter rarely buys a single print on its own, and the shop page she starts from is often a bundle.

The pain points are consistent across forums and shop guides. Colour looks different on screen and depends on the monitor, and fabrics are usually lighter and brighter in person ([Quiltingboard](https://quiltingboard.com/main-f1/color-misrepresentation-online-fabric-t312959.html); [Linda's](https://lindas.com/pages/online-vs-in-store-fabric-buying-quilters)). It's hard to judge how big a print is, or whether fabrics go together. **Cut yardage is final sale almost everywhere**, while unused precuts can often be returned within 30 days ([Gather Here](https://gatherhereonline.com/policies/refund-policy); [Shannon Fabrics](https://shannonfabrics.com/shipping-and-returns)). Shops warn that buying too little is the bigger risk, because the line may have sold out by the time you go back ([Linda's](https://lindas.com/pages/online-vs-in-store-fabric-buying-quilters)). Free shipping starts at **$59 at Missouri Star and $99–100 at other shops** ([Missouri Star](https://missouriquiltco.com/pages/shipping-returns); [eQuilter](https://equilter.com/about)). That nudges people towards one larger order. Connecting Threads' November 2025 figures from Grips (**$4.43 million from 30,891 orders**) work out to an average order of about $140–143 ([Grips](https://gripsintelligence.com/insights/retailers/connectingthreads.com)). Taken together, a tool that shows the actual fabric in the actual quilt, at true scale, with the yardage rounded up, addresses the things this buyer worries about most. A colour-calibrated screen is not on that list, and no visualizer can provide one. Even PreQuilt's reviewer writes that it "narrows the candidates; it does not certify color" ([Omid Saffari](https://omidsaffari.com/blog/prequilt-review-for-fabric-color-planning)).

## A few big shops on Awin, a long tail on Shopify, and 4–10% commissions

Revenue figures for the big online shops are panel estimates that disagree by up to ten times. Grips puts **Fat Quarter Shop at about $10.4 million in November 2025, against $3.5 million for Missouri Star's site and $2.1 million for Shabby Fabrics** ([Grips](https://gripsintelligence.com/insights/retailers/fatquartershop.com)). Similarweb gives Fat Quarter Shop $25–50 million a year ([Similarweb](https://www.similarweb.com/es/company/fatquartershop.com)), and estimates for Missouri Star range from $48 million to $250 million ([ZoomInfo](https://www.zoominfo.com/c/missouri-star-quilt-co/346066566); [LeadIQ](https://leadiq.com/c/missouri-star-quilt-company/5a1d98b82300005c00875b61)). The safest conclusion is that Fat Quarter Shop is probably the largest online-only seller of quilting cotton. Missouri Star is the best-known brand, and its owners also bought LoveCrafts in 2024 ([LoveCrafts](https://www.lovecrafts.com/en-gb/c/article/lovecrafts-group-acquired)). Below these sits a long tail of independent shops. Shopify is their default platform: FabShopNet published a 2026 guide to Shopify for quilt shops, and apps such as Sew Much Commerce let shops sell by fractional yards ([FabShopNet](https://fabshopnet.com/shopify-for-fabric-and-quilt-shops/); [Shopify App Store](https://apps.shopify.com/fabric-shop)). Like Sew is the specialist alternative, combining a shop's till, stock and online store ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/compare/83891-10021014/Shopify-vs-Like-Sew)). No count of shops per platform was found.

Shopify matters for the return link because it documents **cart permalinks** of the form `/cart/{variant_id}:{quantity}`. These links can apply a `discount=` code and can send the buyer to the cart instead of straight to checkout. They also replace whatever is already in the buyer's cart, and links longer than 2,048 bytes fall back to the shop's home page ([shopify.dev](https://shopify.dev/docs/apps/build/checkout/create-cart-permalinks.md)). On a shop that sells fabric in quarter-yard units, "Buy at <shop>" could therefore carry the exact amount Block & Bind's cutting plan worked out, for example seven quarter-yards. How each fractional-yard app models its variants would need checking shop by shop. No public cart-link format was found for Like Sew or the other non-Shopify systems.

The norm, then, is **about 5–10% of the sale**. Shops can afford that. Quilt shops usually price fabric at "keystone", twice the wholesale cost, for roughly a 50% gross margin before sales and shipping ([QuiltingBoard](https://www.quiltingboard.com/main-f1/fabric-prices-t98852.html)). A 6% share therefore takes about an eighth of the shop's gross profit on that fabric. Quiltster takes more: **10% on kits and 15% on fabric sales** ([Quiltster Seller Terms](https://blog.quiltster.com/?p=5155); [Seller Agreement](https://blog.quiltster.com/?p=10470)). Shops accept that rate because Quiltster brings them the buyer. No case was found of a shop paying a design app a share of sales, and PreQuilt has no shop-partner programme that search could find.

## Design-to-buy exists only inside closed catalogues

Very few shops let you see their fabric in a quilt. **eQuilter's Free Pattern Designer** lets shoppers drag fabrics from its own stock into a pattern and add the result to the cart. Saved fabrics appear in a selector, which needs a login ([eQuilter help](https://www.equilter.com/help/free_pattern_designer)). **Quiltster** pairs a subscription planner (about $9.99 a month or $99 a year) with a marketplace where partner shops turn a planned quilt into a kit you can buy. When you check out, it warns you if "there is not enough fabric in stock to make your quilt" and asks you to pick replacements ([Quiltster](https://blog.quiltster.com/?p=4699)). It is tied mostly to Quiltworx patterns. **Quiltler** sells an embeddable designer to fabric companies for $29–79 a month. The embed shows only that company's prints and ends in a shopping list with where-to-buy links. Its demo dashboard shows **324 where-to-buy clicks from 4,634 designer views, about 7%**, but that is a sample dashboard, not verified data ([Quiltler](https://quiltler.com/fabric-companies); [Quiltler demo](https://quiltler.com/partner-demo)). One small shop, Wyldwood Creative, links to a volunteer's mockup tool and asks visitors to donate to its developer ([Wyldwood](https://wyldwoodcreative.com/pages/design-tools)). Searches found no visualizer at Missouri Star or Fat Quarter Shop. They sell through tutorials and kits. Spoonflower's previews show a single print at scale, and Spoonflower itself sells test swatches because screens can't be trusted for colour ([Spoonflower help](https://support.spoonflower.com/hc/en-us/articles/204474630)).

The consumer design tools stop short of the purchase. EQ8 ships about 6,200 manufacturer fabrics and sells new "Stash" packs, PreQuilt matches its swatches to Kona, Moda, Art Gallery and Tilda, and Swatchit matches its colours to Kona names. None of them has a buy button or an affiliate link that search turned up ([EQ shop](https://electricquilt.com/online-shop/); [Stitched in Color](https://www.stitchedincolor.com/blog/2024/7/3/welcome-prequilt)). A September 2026 review calls PreQuilt "not a fabric marketplace" ([Omid Saffari](https://omidsaffari.com/blog/prequilt-review-for-fabric-color-planning)). Fabric manufacturers don't build tools either: Robert Kaufman has a shop locator and Art Gallery has static lookbooks. They supply swatches to third parties instead ([Robert Kaufman](https://www.robertkaufman.com/where_to_buy/); [AGF lookbooks](https://liveartgalleryfabrics.com/fabric-lookbooks/)). So there are two business models. **Consumer apps charge the quilter, and business tools charge the fabric company or take a cut from the shop.** No app was found that does both, or one that works across shops.

Evidence that visualizers raise sales is thin and comes mostly from vendors. Nothing specific to quilting exists beyond Quiltler's demo figure. Furniture and home studies claim "8x", "9x" or "11x" purchase rates, including Roomvo's claim for the fabric and wallpaper seller DecoratorsBest ([Roomvo](https://hello.roomvo.com/gated-case-study-decorators-best)). Those figures compare shoppers who chose to use the tool with shoppers who didn't, so they mostly show that engaged shoppers buy more. The most believable figure is CB2's, reported secondhand: **+7% conversion, +21% revenue per visit and +13% average order value** against pages without AR ([Single Grain](https://www.singlegrain.com/digital-marketing/ar-experiences-that-boosted-conversion-rates-by-189/)). A shop pitch should promise more confidence and fewer wrong orders, then prove a lift with the shop's own numbers, not quote a multiplier. The history adds a warning. Threadbias's community closed in 2015 because running it "has taken quite a toll financially" ([Road Home Quilting](https://roadhomequilting.blogspot.com/2015/08/threadbias-switching-to-quilt-canvas.html)).

## What this means for the "Test it on Block & Bind" button

**The idea fills a real gap.** No tool was found that starts on any shop's product page, puts that fabric into a quilt the quilter designs herself, and sends her back to the same shop to buy it. Block & Bind also has things the closed tools lack. Its 3D drape shows the quilt at true size on a bed, which the earlier competitor research found no other quilt tool doing. Its cutting plan works out yardage, which can become the quantity in the cart. And the app is free, so the quilter pays nothing. The flow also fits code that already exists. Records made while signed out have no `owner`, and signing in already offers to take them into the account. A guest design that becomes the user's on her first save is therefore close to how storage and sync already work (`storage/repo.js`, `storage/sync.js`).

**The commission is the weak link, and the problem is structural.** An affiliate programme pays a publisher for a buyer the publisher sent. With this button the buyer was already on the shop's page, and the "Buy at <shop>" link sends her back. If that return link carries an affiliate tag, the shop pays 6% on a visitor it already had. Any careful shop, and any network that reviews traffic quality, will see this as claiming credit for sales the shop would have made anyway. Short cookies make it worse in the other direction. Fat Quarter Shop's cookie lasts 7 days, and quilters often plan for weeks, so a sale that really came from designing in Block & Bind can lose its attribution before she buys. Off-the-shelf affiliate programmes are a poor fit for both reasons. They suit a different flow, where Block & Bind sends its own users out to buy, for example "Buy this quilt's fabric" from a design started in the app.

**Pitch a partner deal priced on what can be measured.** Three structures fit the evidence. The first is a flat monthly fee for the button and a branded entry, in the range Quiltler charges fabric companies ($29–79 a month). It is predictable for a small shop and needs no tracking. The second is a revenue share only on orders that come back through Block & Bind's cart link, marked with a discount code or a reference the shop can see in its own reports. The norm is 5–10%, and Quiltster's 10–15% is the ceiling, since Quiltster also handles the selling. The third is a free trial period in which the shop compares orders and average order value with and without the button, because no published quilting data proves a lift. The cart link helps here. It carries the exact yardage, so a quilter buys a whole quilt's worth of one fabric at once instead of guessing, and that helps the order clear the $59–100 free-shipping threshold. That lift in order value is the easiest benefit to show a shop. The rough maths is modest: 6% of a $140 order is about **$8.40 an order**, so $1,000 a month takes about 120 attributed orders. Plan the button as a way to get quilters into the app and win shop partners, not as a main income.

**Start with independent Shopify shops, not the giants.** Fat Quarter Shop and Connecting Threads have Awin programmes, but they are big, standardised and unlikely to sign bespoke deals. Missouri Star's programme is by invitation and sells through video. eQuilter already has its own designer. Independent shops on Shopify are the best first partners. They are many, they are under pressure (shops closing, tariffs), they have room in a keystone margin, and their documented cart links make "Buy 1¾ yd at <shop>" possible. Adding the button can start as a plain link with the fabric's image address, name, price, width and repeat size in the URL, plus a small piece of shop code later. Because most quilters still prefer their local shop, a printable or emailed fabric list for non-Shopify shops keeps the feature useful there too.

**Five product points come out of the buyer research.**

| Finding | Implication for the button |
|---|---|
| Quilters buy collections and precuts, rarely one print | Let the shop page pass a bundle or a whole collection, not only "that fabric"; offer to add the quilter's own saved fabrics next to it |
| Print scale is a stated pain, and shop photos rarely give it | The link should carry the fabric's real repeat size or swatch width; without it, a true-scale preview is a guess |
| Colour on screen can't be trusted | Say so plainly ("colours vary by screen") and point to the shop's swatch or sample, as Spoonflower does |
| 34% of online quilting revenue is on mobile | The button opens on phones. The web app's phone path (which already skips the tutorial) must handle this entry, or hand it to a light 2D view |
| Collections sell out fast | Check stock or price when the quilter comes back (Quiltster forces replacements at checkout); expect the cart link to fail sometimes |

**Three conflicts with the current app need the owner's decision.** First, the welcome page no longer offers "Continue without an account". Guest mode for shop visitors reverses that choice for one entry path, so it should be decided deliberately rather than slipped in. Second, the page sets `Referrer-Policy: strict-origin-when-cross-origin` (`app/dist/_headers`). A shop sees only the Block & Bind domain in its referrer reports, never the design, so attribution must travel in the link itself (a code or a reference). Third, putting a shop's fabric photo into the 3D view needs the shop's image server to allow cross-origin use (CORS) for WebGL. Many Shopify image links allow it, but not all shops' images will. Copying the image through Block & Bind's server avoids the problem, at the cost of storage and of permission terms for the print, which belongs to the manufacturer. The partner agreement should cover that use, in the same spirit as the permission still needed for the makers' logos on the welcome page.

## Conclusion

The research turns the plan around. The "Test it" button doesn't bring a shop new customers. It converts the hesitant customers a shop already has, at the point where cut-to-order, no-returns fabric makes hesitation expensive. That is worth paying for, but not through affiliate networks built around new visitors and 7-day cookies. The case to make to shops is measurable confidence: the right fabric, at true scale, in a quilt the buyer designed, with the yardage already in the cart. The deal to offer is a direct partnership with a modest fee or a share of tracked orders, starting with independent Shopify shops. Being shop-neutral is the advantage. eQuilter, Quiltster and Quiltler each lock the quilter into one catalogue, and the quilter can't take her design to another shop.

The market's size argues for keeping this cheap and secondary. A flat $4.5 billion market, an older core buyer who still prefers her local shop, and roughly $8 a tracked order mean shop revenue will grow slowly. The bigger gain may be indirect. Every shop that adds the button sends Block & Bind quilters who are already in the middle of buying, and that kind of new user is the hardest for a free tool to win. Two cheap tests come first, before any code or pitch deck. Ask three or four independent shop owners whether they would pay for this, and which way: a flat fee or a share. And check, on a few real Shopify shops, whether their fabric images load in WebGL and whether a quarter-yard cart permalink works.
